## 1. Introduction: The Paradigm Shift in Public-Key Cryptography and the Existential Crisis

Public-key cryptography, which forms the foundation of the modern digital economy and internet infrastructure, has relied entirely on the high mathematical difficulty of Integer Factorization and the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP). In a classical computing environment, these mathematical problems functioned as an impenetrable fortress, requiring thousands of years of computation time to solve. However, with the development of a "Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer" (CRQC) — a machine capable of running Shor's Algorithm without physical or logical errors — becoming a visible reality, these cryptographic premises are facing a fundamental threat of collapse.

In the past, information security experts and the cryptography community assumed that "Q-Day" (the day quantum computers neutralize modern cryptographic systems) was a theoretical event that would only occur decades into the distant future. However, the timeline for this quantum threat has been drastically shortened in recent years due to advancements in quantum computing hardware, leaps in Quantum Error Correction algorithms, and the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into quantum circuit optimization. Specifically, massive distributed ledger technologies (DLTs) like Bitcoin and Ethereum, which rely on the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) to prove asset ownership and maintain network integrity, are exposed to a severe existential threat where the entire system's security could collapse upon the emergence of a CRQC.

Recognizing the urgency of this threat, major global technology companies like Google and Cloudflare have significantly advanced their target years for completing the migration to Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) to 2029. The Ethereum Foundation has also established a proactive migration roadmap with the same 2029 target. Furthermore, in August 2024, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) officially finalized new quantum-resistant cryptographic standards (FIPS 204, FIPS 205, etc.), urging a transition of global security systems.

Based on recently published top-tier academic research and technological trends, this analysis reevaluates the timeline for the emergence of CRQCs and dissects the specific threat mechanisms that quantum computers pose to the Bitcoin network. In particular, it quantitatively analyzes the possibilities of signature forgery attacks (via Shor's algorithm), mempool front-running attacks, and the neutralization of the mining algorithm (via Grover's algorithm). Additionally, it diagnoses the severe "State Bloat" problem that a transition to quantum-resistant cryptography will cause, and comprehensively examines various protocol upgrades (BIP-360, BIP-361, etc.) and the governance dilemmas surrounding lost assets currently being debated within the Bitcoin ecosystem.

## 2. Rapid Advancements in Quantum Computing and Reevaluating the Q-Day Timeline

### 2.1. Google Quantum AI Research and the Dramatic Reduction in Decryption Resources for secp256k1

Most major cryptocurrency networks, including Bitcoin, use a specific elliptic curve called "secp256k1" within the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) to prove the authenticity of transactions and asset ownership. From a traditional defensive perspective, it was estimated that solving the 256-bit Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) would require millions, if not tens of millions, of physical qubits. This was due to the massive overhead of quantum error correction technologies required to control the noise inevitably generated during quantum operations. Consequently, Bitcoin's signature scheme was considered to have a sufficient security margin against quantum threats in the short term.

However, on March 31, 2026, a paper titled *Securing Elliptic Curve Cryptocurrencies against Quantum Vulnerabilities*, published by the Google Quantum AI research team in collaboration with researchers from the Ethereum Foundation and Stanford University, presented a mathematical breakthrough that completely overturned the academic community's complacent predictions. According to the research, by highly optimizing the quantum circuits implementing Shor's algorithm, the spacetime cost required to solve the discrete logarithm problem decreases by about 10 times compared to previous estimates. Specifically, it was mathematically proven that the signature could be broken using under 1,200 logical qubits and 90 million Toffoli gates, or under 1,450 logical qubits and 70 million Toffoli gates.

This algorithm optimization leads to a dramatic reduction in hardware requirements. Assuming a superconducting quantum architecture based on planar connectivity with a physical error rate of 10^-3, the required number of physical qubits drops from previous estimates (like the Litinski model) of around 9 million to under 500,000 — a nearly 20-fold reduction, making it highly feasible. Furthermore, recent research by the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) and the startup Oratomic confirmed that utilizing a neutral-atom-based architecture could drastically lower the physical qubit requirement even further, down to the 10,000 to 26,000 range.

The most fatal aspect is the computation speed. When using the aforementioned superconducting-based "Fast-clock" architecture, this optimized attack circuit can fully extract a private key from a Bitcoin public key within a few minutes. This timeframe is shorter than Bitcoin's average block generation time of 10 minutes, suggesting that the quantum attack has moved beyond the theoretical stage and entered the realm of practical "transaction front-running." Conversely, while a neutral-atom-based "Slow-clock" architecture requiring roughly 26,000 physical qubits would take days to decrypt the cryptography — making short-term exposure attacks in the mempool impossible — it still poses a critical threat to long-term exposed addresses.

### 2.2. Zero-Knowledge Proof (ZKP) Censorship and the Acceleration of AI-Based Reverse Engineering

The research results from Google's Quantum AI team sent massive shockwaves through the cryptography and blockchain industries, but simultaneously triggered an unprecedented academic censorship incident and a paradox of security. Recognizing the destructive power of their findings, Google coordinated privately with U.S. government agencies. As a result, they decided not to publicly release the detailed quantum circuit designs of the attack vector, to avoid providing a specific manual to malicious hacker states or organizations. This caused a stir when Charles Guillemet, CTO of the security firm Ledger, testified that the U.S. government had blatantly "blocked" the full publication of the paper.

However, Google still needed to mathematically prove that their claims of extreme resource optimization were not fabricated. To do this, they adopted an indirect cryptographic technique called "Zero-Knowledge Proof" (ZKP). A zero-knowledge proof is a technology that proves a proposition is true without revealing any additional information other than the fact that it is true. Google published a zero-knowledge verifier to the academic community, hiding the internal structure of their optimized quantum circuit while only confirming whether a specific Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) point addition operation was successfully performed. This marked the first recorded instance in scientific research history where zero-knowledge proofs were used as a tool for academic censorship and knowledge control.

Yet, this censorship measure designed for security acted as the ultimate backfire, opening a Pandora's box. As Charles Guillemet pointed out, the zero-knowledge verifier Google released inadvertently provided open-source developers and hackers worldwide with a perfect "Reward Function" to instantly test whether their randomly generated quantum circuits matched Google's secret circuit. Distributed intelligence across the globe immediately began reverse engineering it.

The results were shockingly swift. Within just two months, French researcher André Schrottenloher independently built an ECDLP optimization circuit rivaling Google's claims, and even published an improvement that further reduced the gate count by 6.5% to up to 10% compared to Google's undisclosed circuit. Furthermore, as cutting-edge AI technologies entered this optimization race, the situation rapidly escalated. According to an announcement by Sreeram Kannan, founder of EigenCloud, an 18-year-old researcher named Gajesh Naik operated a custom AI Agent Swarm over a single weekend to independently reconstruct 80% of Google's undisclosed breakthrough. Additionally, undergraduate student Gautham Anant achieved roughly a 2x improvement over existing quantum cryptographic circuits. The open-source community, augmented by AI, instantly dismantled the hidden top-secret knowledge through the zero-knowledge proof, and the community officially achieved results exceeding Google's initial optimization level by 11.5% via the ecdsa.fail leaderboard. This incident proved that attempts by governments or massive corporations to monopolize and control knowledge regarding quantum threats for national security reasons are fundamentally impossible in the AI era, dramatically advancing the timeline for the democratization of this threat.

### 2.3. Revised Q-Day Timeline: The 2032 Tipping Point

In the past, the U.S. government and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) conservatively estimated the arrival of Q-Day to be around 2035 and established their cryptographic migration guidelines accordingly. However, top cryptography experts who observed Google's optimization research and the AI-driven reverse engineering incident warn that the existing timeline has become completely meaningless.

Justin Drake, a core researcher at the Ethereum Foundation and a co-author of the Google quantum paper, strongly criticized the U.S. government's 2035 target as "a joke" and drastically revised the timeline. According to Drake's analytical model, the probability of Q-Day — the moment a cryptographically relevant quantum computer cracks the wallet-protecting cryptography on a live blockchain — arriving by 2030 is 10%, and the probability of it arriving by 2032 reaches 50%.

The fundamental terror of Q-Day is that it could happen silently, without any official declaration of war. Even if Bitcoin or Ethereum private keys are mathematically extracted via quantum computation, there is no technical way for the network to distinguish between a transaction by the legitimate owner and a hijacked transaction by a hacker. Driven by the absolute crisis that responses must be completed before the 50% probability tipping point of 2032, entities like Ethereum, Google, and Cloudflare have set 2029 as the final deadline for completing their PQC migration and are focusing entirely on infrastructure transition. Considering the nature of decentralized Bitcoin networks, where hard forks and coordinating millions of nodes can take years, 2029 is by no means a relaxed timeframe.

## 3. Specific Quantum Threat Dynamics and Analysis on the Bitcoin Network

The threat a quantum computer poses to the Bitcoin ecosystem is not simply proportional to the total monetary value of assets a user holds. The severity of the threat is determined entirely by the specific "Address Type" of the Unspent Transaction Output (UTXO) holding the asset, and whether the "Public Key" of that address has a history of being exposed on the blockchain.

The security of Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) is based on the principle that while it is easy to derive a public key by applying scalar multiplication to a base point with a private key, performing the reverse operation to find the private key from only the public key — the ECDLP — is computationally impossible. The only attack vector for a quantum attacker (running Shor's algorithm) is to collect an exposed public key and use a period-finding algorithm via the Quantum Fourier Transform to rapidly solve the discrete logarithm and reverse-calculate the private key. Therefore, the "visibility" of the public key acts as the sole switch for quantum hacking.

### 3.1. Quantum Threat Tiers Based on Public Key Exposure

When analyzing on-chain data and technical address formats, Bitcoin's quantum threat exposure is strictly categorized into three tiers.

**Tier A: Addresses with Immediate and Permanent Public Key Exposure (Long-Range Targets)**

This tier represents the highest level of quantum threat exposure and will be the very first target for hacking immediately upon the emergence of a quantum computer. For these address types, the entire public key is permanently recorded in the blockchain script from the moment funds are received. Therefore, a quantum attacker faces zero time constraints for deriving the private key, and can successfully execute an attack even if they run slow quantum computations over months or years.

- **P2PK (Pay-to-Public-Key):** The very first address format used in the earliest days of the Bitcoin network. P2PK outputs embed the full public key directly into the locking script without passing it through a hashing process. Including the approximately 1.1 million BTC attributed to Satoshi Nakamoto (spread across roughly 22,000 addresses), about 1.7 million BTC globally are stored in this legacy format, and it is presumed that the private keys for most of these have been lost.
- **P2MS (Pay-to-Multisig):** An early, raw form of multi-signature format that exposes all participating public keys on-chain.
- **P2TR (Pay-to-Taproot):** Ironically, Taproot, Bitcoin's newest address format introduced in 2021, is also exposed to long-term threats from a quantum perspective. To improve privacy while designing the Schnorr Signatures-based "Key-path spend" structure, a tweaked public key is included directly inside the output. A quantum attacker can attempt an attack based on this tweaked public key.

**Tier B: Addresses with Post-Exposure of Public Keys Due to User Behavior Errors (Behavior-Based Long-Range Exposure)**

Most modern address formats, ranging from P2PKH (Pay-to-Public-Key-Hash) to the latest Native SegWit formats like P2WPKH and P2WSH, inherently hide the public key safely behind double hash functions (SHA-256 and RIPEMD-160). Because hash functions are one-way structures and are not targets of Shor's algorithm, private keys cannot be derived from public key hashes alone. However, careless cryptocurrency management behaviors by users can degrade these safe addresses into long-term threat targets identical to Tier A.

- **Address Reuse:** To "spend" the balance of a specific address in the Bitcoin protocol, the unlocking data must include the user's public key and signature to prove ownership and be broadcasted to the network. If funds are partially spent and the change is received back to the same address, or if an already-spent address is "reused" for receiving deposits, the remaining balance of that address is left defenseless behind a public key that has already been exposed to the world.
- **Fork Spending:** In the process of spending duplicated UTXOs on a past Bitcoin hard fork chain (like Bitcoin Cash, BCH), a fatal error can occur where the public key of the identical address on the main chain (BTC) is indirectly exposed.
- **Extended Public Key (xpub) Exposure:** Carelessly sharing xpub data to generate watch-only wallets for accounting or tracking in exchanges or wallet software can allow the calculation of specific derivation paths in the quantum era, collapsing the security of the entire wallet.

**Tier C: Addresses with Short-Term Exposure Only in the Mempool at the Time of Spending (Short-Range Threat)**

Even a perfect custodian who maintains strict security hygiene and always uses single-use receiving addresses is not entirely free from quantum threats. The moment a user signs a transaction and broadcasts it to the network to transfer funds, that transaction sits in the mempool (an unconfirmed transaction waiting room) until a miner includes it in a block. During this time, the public key is broadcast to the entire network along with the unlocking script.

During this brief window, it is theoretically possible for a quantum attacker to scan the mempool, hijack the exposed public key, run Shor's algorithm in an instant to derive the private key, forge a hijacked transaction with a significantly higher miner fee (Replace-by-Fee, RBF) than the original owner, and force it into the block first. This is known as "transaction hijacking" or an "on-spend front-running" attack.

The danger of Tier C depends entirely on the clock speed of the quantum hardware. The Google paper clearly distinguishes quantum architectures into "Slow-clock" (neutral atom, ion trap-based) and "Fast-clock" (superconducting, photon-based). Slow-clock models take hours or days to compute, making mempool attacks impossible, but a superconducting Fast-clock architecture can break signatures in a few minutes, possessing the destructive power to perfectly execute a front-running attack within Bitcoin's 10-minute block interval.

### 3.2. Asset Custody and Massive Potential Damage Scale

Applying these vulnerability metrics to global Bitcoin liquidity makes the severity of the situation even clearer. Synthesizing a 2025 report from Chaincode Labs and a 2026 analysis by Ark Invest, the volume of assets where public keys are already permanently exposed on-chain (Tier A and Tier B) due to address reuse or format characteristics like P2PK and P2TR reaches between 6.26 million and up to 6.8 million coins. This is an astronomical scale, accounting for roughly 25% to 35% of the total circulating Bitcoin supply.

If quantum computers begin decrypting wallets on this massive scale, a single hijacking event could trigger hundreds of billions of dollars in dumping, physically paralyzing the market and collapsing the trust in the system's integrity, which is the intrinsic value of cryptocurrency.

Furthermore, the form of asset custody acts as an important variable in quantum threat resilience. Bitcoin asset management is broadly divided into "Self-Custody" using hardware devices like Trezor or Ledger, "Third-Party Custody" where private keys are entirely delegated to a third-party exchange (as seen in the Mt. Gox or FTX incidents), and "Multi-Institution Custody (MIC)," which has recently emerged as an alternative. In a third-party custody environment, exchanges manage large-scale UTXOs via centralized omnibus wallets. Consequently, regardless of the customer's intent, massive capital carries the risk of being exposed to quantum threats en masse due to address reuse or inefficient UTXO management by the exchange.

### 3.3. Bitcoin Mining Algorithm (SHA-256) and the Reality of Grover's Quantum Attack

Distinct from the threat of quantum computers collapsing digital signature schemes (Shor's algorithm), the threat aimed at Bitcoin's core Proof-of-Work (PoW) mining algorithm has also been a subject of continuous academic debate. This attack utilizes "Grover's Algorithm," which dramatically shortens the search speed of massive unstructured databases.

Bitcoin mining is a brute-force search operation that repeatedly applies hashing (SHA-256) to the block header to find a value below a specific Difficulty Target required by the network. If a classical computer needs an average of N operations (up to 2^256) to find the answer in a 256-bit hash space, a quantum computer equipped with Grover's algorithm provides a theoretical quadratic speedup, finding the answer with only √N (about 2^128) quantum operations. Theoretically, it seems quantum miners could use this massive speed advantage to overwhelm traditional classical mining pools and monopolize the blockchain.

However, according to the latest analyses by experts, the practical threat quantum mining (Grover) poses to Bitcoin is virtually negligible compared to the immediate threat of signature decryption. There are clear physical and economic constraints to this.

| Feature Comparison | Classical ASIC Miner | Quantum Mining (Grover's Algorithm) | Comparative Analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operation Speed | Requires approx. 2^255 operations | Requires approx. 2^128 quantum ops. | Theoretical operation count is overwhelmingly superior for quantum. |
| Parallelization | Near-infinite linear parallel expansion possible with hundreds of thousands of cores | Large-scale parallelization is mathematically nearly impossible due to sequential quantum circuit traits | The parallel throughput of ASICs overwhelms the gap in quantum efficiency. |
| Physical Resources & Energy | MW-scale power consumption | Over 10^23 physical qubits and 10^25 Watts of power | Requires Kardashev Scale Type II civilization-level energy (unrealizable). |
| Hardware Control Infrastructure | Cheap silicon-based design & room-temperature operation | Cryogenic cooling, ultra-precise laser or microwave pulse controllers | From a CAPEX standpoint, quantum mining is completely unrealistic. |

A rigorous quantum hardware simulation study published in 2026 statistically proved the impracticality of quantum mining. According to this open-source simulator, even under the most favorable attack conditions (partial preimage, 32-bit difficulty), a minimum of 10^8 physical qubits and national-grid-level power are consumed. If the actual Bitcoin mainnet difficulty as of early 2025 (approx. 79 bits) is applied, the required resources explode to 10^23 qubits and a planetary-scale energy of 10^25 Watts. In other words, the mere algebraic reduction in operation counts can never offset the physical hardware clock speed limitations and overhead. Mining security possesses a far stronger resilience against quantum advancements than signature security.

## 4. The Severe Dilemma of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) Transition: Network State Bloat

As the emergence of ultra-fast quantum computers becomes imminent, transitioning cryptographic systems is no longer an option but a prerequisite for survival. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), which sets the standard for global cryptography, officially finalized FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, formerly SPHINCS+), and the key encapsulation mechanism FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) as the ultimate standards for post-quantum cryptography in August 2024, after a long process of public competition and verification.

The strongest candidate to replace the signature algorithms of blockchains like Bitcoin is the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature algorithm, ML-DSA. However, the process of completely stripping away the existing efficient elliptic curves (ECDSA) and transplanting this new, heavy PQC system into a decentralized network like Bitcoin, which has extremely limited bandwidth, triggers a massive technical disaster known as "State Bloat."

### 4.1. Quantitative Analysis of the Explosive Increase in Physical and Algorithmic Sizes

PQC systems use much larger and more complex mathematical lattice polynomials to evade quantum computation. If the NIST standard ML-DSA-65 parameter set is integrated into Bitcoin, the resulting increase in data structure sizes is analyzed as follows:

- **Public Key Size:** The compressed public key for secp256k1 currently used in Bitcoin is merely 33 bytes. However, the ML-DSA-65 public key balloons to 1,952 bytes, which is a 59x explosion compared to the existing size.
- **Signature Size:** The digital signature essential for validating transactions also grows massively from the existing 71 bytes to 3,309 bytes, roughly a 47x increase.
- **Typical Transaction Footprint:** Consequently, the size of a typical Bitcoin transaction with one input and two outputs swells from an average of 250 bytes to approximately 2,800 bytes. This implies that the transaction throughput capable of fitting into the same 1MB block (4MWU weight) plummets to about one-eleventh.

This expansion in physical data size doesn't just consume network bandwidth; it directly impacts the Unspent Transaction Output (UTXO) database that Bitcoin nodes must maintain constantly in memory. As the state storage requirement per account also grows 59-fold from 33 bytes to 1,952 bytes, the total network UTXO set capacity is estimated to permanently expand from the current \~5GB level to a deformed size of 296GB. Since past UTXO data cannot be arbitrarily pruned (deleted) without compromising the blockchain's integrity, this massive storage overhead is permanently passed on to node operators.

### 4.2. Node Ecosystem Centralization Risks and Governance Dilemma

This state bloat delivers a fatal blow to Bitcoin's philosophy of decentralization. The time required to newly synchronize a Full Node will skyrocket from a matter of days to several weeks, the total storage requirement will surge from the current \~1TB to over 5–10TB, and the synchronization bandwidth requirement will also spike by more than 10 times. According to recent research analyses, the pressure from these infrastructure costs and management burdens is projected to cause between 50% to up to 80% of currently globally distributed voluntary node operators to drop out, triggering a severe chain reaction leading to network centralization.

The biggest problem lies in the complex decision-making mechanism (governance) of the Bitcoin ecosystem. Even though past Bitcoin upgrades like SegWit and Taproot had clear advantages of lowering transaction fees and improving network capacity, it still took over 5 years just to achieve 50% adoption through the consensus of miners and nodes across the entire network.

In contrast, a PQC migration is a "purely defensive downgrade" that imposes immediate and obvious pain, such as dropping system capacity by more than half and causing user transaction fees to skyrocket by 2–3 times. The probability of an autonomous blockchain ecosystem rapidly agreeing to and voluntarily embracing such an upgrade with no immediate cost-benefit is extremely low. Considering historical patterns, reaching a consensus alone is expected to take 10–15 years, but this directly collides with Justin Drake's predicted 2032 Q-Day timeline, driving the Bitcoin network into a catastrophic bottleneck.

### 4.3. Protocol-Level Optimization Strategies

To mitigate the shock of state bloat, security technology researchers are designing protocol optimization measures.

- **Hybrid Signatures:** It is dangerous to secure trillions of dollars in value on a single structure of a new quantum-resistant algorithm that has not yet been field-tested long-term. Therefore, a hybrid system is being heavily considered, bundling classical elliptic curves like secp256k1 with PQC elements like ML-DSA-65, creating a dual safety net so that if one algorithm is breached, the other maintains the defense line.
- **SegWit-Style Integration (Extension Blocks):** By detaching the massive PQC public keys and signatures from the main block space and isolating them in a separate "Extension Block" format (Witness data), compatibility for legacy nodes is maintained, aiming for a capacity preservation effect of about 20–25%. BTQ's testnet also restores the SegWit discount for this reason, to offset the cost shock of large signatures.
- **Batch Verification:** Instead of individually verifying multiple signatures, bundling them into a single batch operation for verification is being strongly discussed as a way to offset computational overhead by about 15–20%. Theoretical research is also running in parallel to dramatically reduce capacity (up to 10x) using ZK-STARK-based compression techniques or state proof trees, but this still entails more than 5 years of time and uncertainty before practical deployment.

## 5. Bitcoin Protocol Innovations for Quantum Resistance: BIP Proposals and Limitations Analysis

To combat Bitcoin's quantum vulnerabilities, various developer and cryptographer groups have continuously proposed Bitcoin Improvement Proposals (BIPs). Among them, we analyze two core protocol proposals showing the most practical and realistic progress (BIP-360, Turnstile structure) alongside the technical alternatives from the Ethereum camp.

### 5.1. BIP-360 (P2MR): Fundamentally Blocking Long-Exposure Threats

To defend the "Long Exposure" assets that quantum computers will target first, researchers including Hunter Beast, Ethan Heilman, and Isabel Foxen Duke have proposed a soft fork-based BIP-360. This proposal introduces an innovative output type called "Pay-to-Merkle-Root (P2MR)," which uses a new prefix 'bc1z'.

The core architectural design of P2MR is that while it reuses the codebase of Taproot (P2TR) defined in the existing BIP 341, it completely excises the "Key-path spend" mechanism — the primary vector for quantum threats — from the protocol structure. Existing Taproot commits an internal public key to the output by tweaking it with the root of a Merkle tree so it appears as a single signature on the surface. However, this method left a mathematical shadow of the public key inside the output, acting as a clue for quantum attacks.

In contrast, P2MR does not commit an internal public key at all, committing purely the final hash of the script tree (Tapbranch hash). To spend a P2MR output, the Control Block of the Merkle path and the Leaf script must be provided, and a specific parity bit in the control block is set to explicitly indicate that no key is used. Consequently, under the P2MR structure, no form of public key is permanently recorded on the blockchain until an actual spend occurs and the script is submitted on-chain, depriving a quantum attacker of the initial data (input) needed for computations.

Currently, the Canadian quantum security startup BTQ Technologies has successfully launched its own testnet, 'Bitcoin Quantum' (v0.3.0), based on this BIP-360 draft. For rapid feedback, this testnet is configured with a 1-minute block interval, applies a 5 BTQ mining reward, and activates the fully NIST-standardized Dilithium (ML-DSA) signature opcode within tapscript to simulate quantum-resistant capabilities in a real environment. The introduction of P2MR has the advantage of plugging the largest security hole while requiring only minimal modifications to the codebases of existing exchanges and wallet ecosystems. However, because it does not completely prevent "Short Exposure Attacks" within the mempool, it will serve as a powerful first-stage defense wall until a final PQC implementation.

### 5.2. Zero-Knowledge STARK Turnstile: Atomic Migration

Even if BIP-360 and new PQC signatures are introduced, the challenge remains of securely moving the 13.5 million assets currently dormant in legacy addresses to new PQC wallets without exposing them to attackers (migration). This is because authorizing the transaction with a private key inevitably exposes the key.

To overcome this paradox, researchers from the digital asset custody institution Anchorage Digital proposed a theoretical one-way migration protocol called the "Turnstile" structure, combining the quantum-resistant nature of hashes with zero-knowledge proofs. Furthermore, in April 2026, Olaoluwa "Roasbeef" Osuntokun, CTO of Lightning Labs, succeeded in actually implementing Bitcoin's first zero-knowledge (zk-STARK) recovery prototype based on BIP-32 seed knowledge, in a similar vein to Anchorage's proposal. This relies on the physical principle that hash functions (like SHA-256) are not targets of Shor's algorithm and possess massive defensive strength even against Grover's algorithm, keeping hash values safe in the quantum era.

Instead of submitting a public key to a blockchain node, the user generates a zero-knowledge-based ZK-STARK (Scalable Transparent Arguments of Knowledge) proof in an off-chain environment. This proves that they fully control the classical private key (sk) that is the original source of that hash value (on-chain commitment). Simultaneously, inside this proof computation, a standard Key Derivation Function (HKDF) is run to deterministically derive a new Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) public key from the owner's classical private key.

The core innovation of this technology is that this entire process is handled as an Atomic Re-Registration within a single transaction. Not only is the owner's private key never leaked to the network, but because the key derivation is mathematically and uniquely determined, it fundamentally blocks "Race condition" (key substitution) attacks where a hacker intercepts the private key mid-process and attempts to forcefully register a random PQC key different from the owner's. Furthermore, the owner can still recover the migrated new PQC wallet using just the seed phrase they originally wrote on paper, maximizing operational stability. Osuntokun's prototype takes about 55 seconds to generate a proof of ownership on an Apple Silicon environment, and the resulting proof file size is around 1.7MB. However, since STARK proof data sizes like this still exceed or heavily burden Bitcoin block processing limits, on-chain realization is only possible if additional maturation in techniques like rollup computations or recursive composition compression occurs.

### 5.3. Hash-Based Signatures (XMSS) and Technical Contrast with the Ethereum Ecosystem

Unlike Bitcoin, Ethereum, which employs an Account-based state management system, is adopting a different approach under the leadership of Justin Drake. Avoiding the complexity of lattice-based systems, the Ethereum camp has set a roadmap to transition to a Hash-based Cryptography signature scheme, whose quantum resistance has been perfectly mathematically proven.

Ethereum's roadmap is preparing a combination of XMSS (Extended Merkle Signature Scheme) — which compresses the existing WOTS+ (Winternitz One-Time Signature) into a multi-tree structure — and the Poseidon2 hash function. XMSS, specified by RFC 8391, places multiple single-use WOTS signatures as leaf nodes in a Merkle tree and highly compresses them into an L-tree structure to generate a single collision-resistant public key.

However, the decisive reason the Bitcoin camp is reluctant to adopt XMSS is State Management (State Dependency). To maintain a secure signature under the XMSS scheme, the user must absolutely store and manage the "State" locally — specifically, which index of the signature tree they have spent. While node operators might have sufficient resources to keep this massive state tree in memory, for regular users with Externally Owned Accounts (EOAs) — especially in cold storage or Hardware Security Module (HSM) environments — failing to synchronize this state leads to a vulnerability where the wallet's security is completely compromised. Therefore, Bitcoin has a path dependency favoring NIST's ML-DSA or SLH-DSA, which are Stateless, even if the computations are relatively heavier.

## 6. The Collapse of Bitcoin's Philosophy: Lost Coins and the 'Burn vs. Steal' Dilemma

The technical migration mechanisms mentioned above are valid solutions only for "active owners" (about 65% of active assets) who still have secure access to their private keys. The true disaster facing Bitcoin lies in the massive 35% of lost and abandoned assets where voluntary migration is physically impossible. Particularly, the 1.1 million BTC sleeping in Satoshi Nakamoto's wallets have all their public keys fully exposed under the P2PK scheme, and by Q-Day, these massive assets will act as an astronomical "Quantum Bounty" for quantum hackers.

### 6.1. The Introduction of BIP-361: The Rationale for Forced Legacy Sunset and Fund Freezing

To prevent such a global economic shock, renowned Bitcoin cypherpunk and engineer Jameson Lopp, along with a team of five researchers (Christian Papathanasiou, Ian Smith, Joe Ross, etc.), officially proposed BIP-361 (Post Quantum Migration and Legacy Signature Sunset) to establish a forceful response mechanism within the Bitcoin protocol.

BIP-361 is not a simple code upgrade, but proposes radical "Sunset" measures that forcefully restrict the use of vulnerable addresses, including the estimated 1.7 million lost P2PK Bitcoins stored in specific addresses. The core framework of the proposal is divided into the following two phases:

- **Phase A:** Triggers approximately 3 years (160,000 blocks) after the soft fork activation. Through network node consensus, the act of sending new funds to quantum-vulnerable addresses (Legacy ECDSA-based) that haven't yet migrated is structurally disallowed at the protocol level. Existing addresses can still send funds but are blocked from receiving new ones, strongly pressuring users to migrate to PQC address types.
- **Phase B:** Triggers about 2 years after Phase A. From this point forward, the spending of all unspent coins dormant in vulnerable addresses via general ECDSA and Schnorr signatures is completely frozen. Only spending requests that pass a strict quantum-safe Rescue protocol (Phase C, ZK proof-based seed recovery) are conditionally permitted, blocking quantum hackers from arbitrarily stealing and using existing keys.

The pro-freeze camp, including Jameson Lopp, argues that such extreme measures are inevitable. If quantum technology falls into the hands of malicious actors (hackers, hostile nation-states, etc.), they could dump the Satoshi stash and others, destroying the entire price defense line of the Bitcoin ecosystem and market.

### 6.2. Clash with Core Bitcoin Philosophy and Hard Fork Risks

However, the BIP-361 proposal has sparked an ideological division rarely seen in the history of the Bitcoin community, triggering the governance dilemma of "Burn vs. Steal."

**Rebuttal from the "Steal" (Leave it) Camp:** The act of a third party (developer consensus) arbitrarily freezing or invalidating (burning) coins in a specific address so they cannot be used is a denial of Bitcoin's fundamentals. Bitcoin is built upon immutable "Absolute Property Rights" that disallow censorship, rely on decentralization, and reject infringements on individual sovereignty. They vehemently argue that even if a tragedy occurs where hackers steal coins using a quantum computer, the act of the protocol altering its own rules to confiscate assets would permanently destroy the trust in the network's eternal immutability, leading to far more fatal consequences. They criticize this as being tantamount to central bank-style control.

This governance debate has escalated beyond the realms of mathematics and cryptography into foundational issues dealing with Bitcoin's political philosophy and the economic redistribution of wealth. If the community fails to narrow the differences in opinion regarding the response method and reaches the Q-Day point around 2030, Bitcoin is highly likely to face a massive "Hard Fork" crisis. This would entail severe hashrate fragmentation — exceeding the past Bitcoin Cash (BCH) split — permanently dividing the network into a quantum-resistant blockchain and a blockchain adhering to classical ideologies.

## 7. Conclusion and Strategic Recommendations for the Quantum Era

Synthesizing the analysis in this report, the timing for the emergence of cryptographically relevant, large-scale quantum computers (CRQC) has escaped the heavily secured physical laboratories and entered the pursuit range of open-source communities allied with AI technology. Google's research reducing qubit resource requirements by 20x and the instant reverse-engineering breakthroughs by AI agents have forced a comprehensive revision of the expiration date of the global cryptographic system. Q-Day should no longer be defined as a theoretical threat post-2035, but as an immediate and realistic ticking time bomb capable of dealing a fatal blow to the Bitcoin network between 2029 and 2032.

Within the Bitcoin network, there exists a fatal structural vulnerability consisting of 6.8 million lost and Satoshi coins that cannot voluntarily request protection, as well as inevitable constant exposure within the mempool. However, if the market and node operators, gripped by fear, hastily migrate to a Post-Quantum Cryptography system that has not yet undergone complete technical verification, the side effects of centralization — driven by the inability to handle a 59x permanent State Bloat — could trigger a catastrophe even greater than quantum hacking itself.

To ensure the complete preservation of the digital asset market and a soft landing for the blockchain ecosystem, the following three phased strategies must be prioritized:

1. **Immediate Strengthening of Personal Security Management (Custody) and Hygiene:** The core of defending against quantum attacks lies in "blocking public key visibility." Individuals and Multi-Institution Custody (MIC) entities must strictly prohibit even a single instance of address reuse when spending assets, and must migrate to modern shelters like Native SegWit (P2WPKH, P2WSH) that hide public keys behind hashes. Furthermore, indirect security exposures via xpub sharing must be blocked to safely preserve assets within the short-term threat tier (Tier C).
2. **Introduction of Crypto-Agility Architecture to Bitcoin Core:** Given the extremely conservative update nature of Bitcoin, rather than immediately implanting PQC like ML-DSA, it is urgent to build a Crypto-Agility infrastructure that can flexibly swap and accommodate various signature schemes. The Extension Block structure of BIP-360 (P2MR) or the zero-knowledge STARK Turnstile compression model proposed by Anchorage Digital should be actively elevated from the BTQ testnet level to the mainnet implementation stage, constructing a technical fortress in advance to block long-exposure threats.
3. **Redefining the Decentralization Philosophy of Governance and Coordinating Communication:** The worst-case risk stems not from the absence of technology, but from the absence of human consensus. The "Burn vs. Steal" debate surrounding the handling of lost coins, and the acceptance of BIP-361 stipulating forced freezing, act as detonators capable of causing a fatal fragmentation (Hard Fork) of the hashrate. Network participants must transcend ideological conflicts to reach an early consensus on autonomous survival guidelines and imprint trust upon the market. Only then can they prevent the scenario where Satoshi's 1.1 million capital degrades into a catalyst that collapses the market.

The future quantum threat is no longer stagnant, bound by the laws of physics. It is a dynamic entity continuously accelerating and optimizing through the convergent capabilities of artificial intelligence and open-source hacker collectives. For the fragmented distributed ledger ecosystem to fully preserve the value of its assets against this incoming tide, a swift response innovation of a new paradigm — transcending the loose consensus governance of the past — is desperately required.
