# A Structural Shift in the Crypto Regulatory Landscape: Project Classification Analysis Based on the Clarity Act's Legislative Structure and Jurisdictional Integration

## TL;DR

• **Legislative status:** House passed July 2025; Senate Banking Committee approved May 14, 2026 (15–9, including two Democratic yes votes); full Senate vote pending.

• **Not threshold-based:** Jurisdiction turns on **rebuttable presumption + certification**, **qualitative coordinated control**, and **Regulation Crypto compliance costs**—not simple 20%/49% ownership tests.

• **CFTC path:** Issuers certify end of managerial efforts + absence of unilateral control; SEC has 60 days to object; otherwise token becomes a **digital commodity**.

• **Winners vs. losers:** Winners — L1s passing certification (or ETF safe harbor if retained), Section 105 codification candidates (XRP), DeFi under Sec. 601 + activity rewards. Losers — centralized teams under Regulation Crypto caps; CeFi passive stablecoin yield models.

## 1. Introduction: Securing Regulatory Clarity and Restructuring the Market

Over the past decade, the U.S. virtual asset market has grown amidst a regulatory environment characterized by jurisdictional disputes between regulatory agencies over the legal nature of assets and **"Regulation by Enforcement."** The regulatory uncertainty that arose from mechanically applying existing securities laws (the Howey Test) to new blockchain technologies delayed the market entry of traditional financial institutions and caused innovative companies to move offshore.

To resolve this deadlock, the **"Digital Asset Market Clarity Act of 2025"** (hereinafter the **Clarity Act**), after passing the House in July 2025, was approved by the **Senate Banking Committee on May 14, 2026**, with **15 votes in favor and 9 against** (including yes votes from two Democratic Senators, Ruben Gallego and Angela Alsobrooks), and is now awaiting a full Senate vote.

This report analyzes the regulatory integration scenarios of projects based on the Senate Banking Committee's approved draft (May 14, 2026). It uses the Act's three actual pillars—not simple ownership percentage thresholds, but rather the **Rebuttable Presumption and Certification process**, the **qualitative Coordinated Control evaluation**, and the **regulatory compliance costs of Regulation Crypto**.

## 2. The Clarity Act's Asset Classification Framework and Jurisdictional Standards

The Clarity Act separates jurisdiction between the **SEC** (Securities and Exchange Commission) and the **CFTC** (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) through a **procedural proof** aligned with the digital asset's lifecycle, rather than mechanically dividing asset characteristics based on specific numerical thresholds (e.g., 20% or 49%).

### 2.1. Digital Commodity Certification Process and Qualitative Coordinated Control Determination

According to **Section 102** of the Act, all network tokens are basically presumed to be **"Ancillary Assets (securities)"** whose value depends on the **"entrepreneurial or managerial efforts"** of developers (Rebuttable Presumption). For this asset to be recognized as a **digital commodity** under CFTC jurisdiction, the issuer or intermediary must **certify** the following requirements to the SEC:

1. **Proof of End of Managerial Efforts:** The issuer or intermediary must prove that the founding team's entrepreneurial and managerial efforts for the project have substantially ended.

2. **Absence of Qualitative Control (Coordinated Control):** No specific single entity or group should have the unilateral/practical control to alter or censor the network.

3. **DAO and Emergency Measure Exceptions (Sec. 104):** The Act does not treat decentralized governance systems (DAOs) themselves as a single controlling entity. Furthermore, it qualitatively stipulates that predefined, limited cybersecurity emergency measures or routine/administrative actions of the system do not constitute **"coordinated control"** unless monopolized by a single actor.

If the issuer submits a certification containing the above information, the SEC has a **60-day review period**. If it does not object with reasonable grounds, the token is converted into a digital commodity.

### 2.2. Section 103 "Regulation Crypto" and Section 104 Resale Restrictions

Early-stage projects that have not yet proven functional decentralization and remain under the **ancillary asset** jurisdiction can legally raise funds through the newly established **"Regulation Crypto"** exemption clause in Section 103. However, this comes with clear ceilings and disclosure obligations.

| Requirement | Detail |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Fundraising Limit** | Up to **$50 million annually** (or 10% of outstanding ancillary asset value, whichever is greater); **$200 million** lifetime cumulative cap |
| **Continuous Disclosure** | Initial disclosures + **semi-annual** financial/operational filings to the SEC |
| **Insider Resale (Sec. 104)** | Related Persons' public resale of ancillary assets **strictly limited for 12 months** to prevent manipulation and dumping |

### 2.3. Section 404: Prohibition of Passive Stablecoin Interest and Allowance of Activity-Based Rewards

The stablecoin yield regulation (**Section 404**), which was one of the core contentious issues of the Clarity Act, was resolved through a compromise by Senators **Thom Tillis** and **Angela Alsobrooks**.

* **Prohibited:** Virtual asset service providers paying **passive yield** economically or functionally equivalent to bank deposits for merely holding stablecoins.

* **Permitted:** Rewards linked to **bona fide activities** on the network—payments, remittances, DeFi liquidity provision, governance voting.

## 3. Projects Expected to Experience Structural Benefits and CFTC Jurisdictional Integration

Under the structure of the Clarity Act, the groups receiving the greatest benefits are:

1. Assets potentially protected by the **"ETF safe harbor"** avoiding certification review altogether
2. **Layer-1** assets demonstrating functional decentralization sufficient to defend the SEC's 60-day review
3. Assets for which previous court rulings are codified under **Section 105**
4. **DeFi protocols** meeting the software developer safe harbor of **Section 601** and activity-based reward requirements of **Section 404**

### 3.1. Assets Potentially Benefiting from the ETF Safe Harbor, Certification, and Section 105

#### The "ETF Safe Harbor" Automatic Exemption (BTC / ETH)

The Senate discussion draft (January 2026) included a provision stating that a token will not be considered an ancillary asset if, on **January 1, 2026**, it was the principal asset of an exchange-traded product (ETP) listed on a U.S. national securities exchange.

> **Note:** Whether this provision survived into the May 14 Banking Committee-approved text requires confirmation against the enrolled bill text. If adopted, Bitcoin and Ethereum would bypass the SEC certification process entirely and be automatically granted digital commodity status.

#### Defending the Certification Process (Other L1s)

For major networks not covered by the ETF safe harbor, they must undergo the **60-day SEC certification review**. Assets where no single entity holds unilateral admin-key authority and that can clearly prove the **end of managerial efforts** through broad distribution of nodes and validators are highly likely to pass this procedural test.

#### Ripple (XRP) and the Application of Section 105

**Section 105** of the Senate amendment stipulates that it prevents the SEC from determining that a digital asset is a security if, prior to enactment, a U.S. court determined in a **non-appealable final judgment** that the digital asset was not a security.

This can be interpreted as a key mechanism to codify **Judge Analisa Torres's ruling**—which did not view XRP trading in the secondary market as securities trading—into federal law.

> **Legal caveat:** Since Section 105 strictly requires the ruling to be in a **non-appealable final state**, whether the Torres ruling meets this requirement needs separate legal verification. If met, Section 105 becomes the basis for resolving the core legal uncertainty XRP faced.

### 3.2. DeFi Developer Protection and Activity-Based Structured Protocols

**Section 601** (Protecting Software Developers) explicitly excludes purely software development and distribution activities, compiling network transactions, or providing computational work for distributed ledgers from federal and state securities laws (**Safe Harbor**), protecting DeFi infrastructure construction.

Sections **301** and **308** clearly define the qualitative definition of DeFi protocols and risk management obligations of centralized intermediaries, separating roles within the ecosystem.

Under the **activity-based rewards** exception of Section 404, DeFi protocols with advanced liquidity provision and structuring models—not simple interest payments—are expected to emerge as legal pathways for institutional capital.

| Protocol Name | Operating Model Features | Positioning within the Legislative Structure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pendle** | Interest rate swap architecture separating assets into Principal Tokens (PT) and Yield Tokens (YT) | Highly likely to fit activity-based swap/derivatives classification; advantageous for institutional RWA structured products |
| **Morpho** | Permissionless lending with differentiated risk control parameters | Institutions can create isolated pools aligned with licensed regulatory scopes |
| **Sky** (formerly MakerDAO) | USDS issuance based on reserves and profit distribution | Attempting to prove activity-based exception via profits from physical reserves (e.g., U.S. Treasuries) |
| **Centrifuge / Maple** | RWA tokenization and institutional on-chain credit desks | Full KYC/due diligence infrastructure for institutional on-chain credit entry |

## 4. Projects Facing Increased Regulatory Inclusion Costs and Operational Risks

Projects that fail to pass the Clarity Act's certification process must bear disclosure obligations and strict constraints equivalent to existing securities laws.

### 4.1. Projects Unable to Prove End of Managerial Efforts and Dependent on Regulation Crypto

Tokens where the founding team or foundation retains **unilateral network update authority**, or where value appreciation entirely depends on the team's marketing and development capabilities, will remain under the SEC's **ancillary asset** jurisdiction.

They must use **Regulation Crypto** under Section 103 and are bound by:

* **$50 million** annual fundraising cap (**$200 million** lifetime)
* **Semi-annual** transparent financial and operational disclosures
* **12-month** insider supply restriction under Section 104

### 4.2. CeFi Platforms Centered on Passive Yield

According to **Section 404**, centralized lending platforms (CeFi) or offshore services paying **fixed interest** similar to bank deposits solely for holding stablecoins—without clear network contributions (remittances, liquidity provision)—will essentially become unable to operate legally within the U.S.

Business models that cannot prove the basis for interest rate generation will inevitably face restructuring.

## 5. Conclusion: Market Restructuring Centered on Procedural Certification and Transparency

The Clarity Act redefines virtual asset regulatory jurisdiction not with simple ownership percentage thresholds, but with a qualitative framework of **"Certification of the end of managerial efforts"** and **"Absence of Unilateral Control."**

### Primary Beneficiaries

* Decentralized infrastructure (**L1**) passing certification—or potentially the **ETF safe harbor** (pending final text)
* Assets resolving legal uncertainty via **Section 105** (notably **XRP**, subject to final-judgment verification)
* Regulatory-friendly **DeFi** meeting **Section 601** developer safe harbor and **Section 404** activity-based exceptions

### Under Pressure

* Projects exercising **centralized management** subject to **Regulation Crypto** caps and semi-annual disclosure
* Platforms relying on **opaque passive yield** models

The market is shifting from enforcement-driven ambiguity toward **procedural certification and transparency** as the defining axis of U.S. digital asset regulation.
